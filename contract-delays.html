The delay problem

A delay is not a pause. It is a decision that gets made for us. And the longer it runs, the less say we have in it.

When negotiations run past the expiration date, work does not stop and neither does the clock. The old agreement holds, improvements wait, and if talks are declared deadlocked the employer gains the right to put its own final terms into effect. None of that requires anyone to act in bad faith. It is simply what happens when a contract is late.

Ours carries one wrinkle worth knowing up front. It does not simply lapse — unless one side puts written notice in the other’s hands by May 2, 2027, it renews for another full year on the terms we already have. The dates further down this page are the ones that decide which of those happens.

The mechanics

Three things happen when a contract runs past its date.

None of them are dramatic on the day they start. That is exactly why they are easy to miss until the cost has already been paid.

01

The old terms keep running

Pay rates, benefits, work rules, scheduling — everything continues as written in the expiring agreement. You are not working without protection. But you are working under terms that were negotiated for a different year, against different costs.

02

Improvements wait

Wage increases and benefit changes generally do not take effect until a new agreement is ratified. They are commonly made retroactive once it is — but retroactive pay arrives later, in one lump, after months of the higher cost of living has already come out of your pocket.

03

At impasse, the employer can move

If the two sides reach a genuine legal deadlock, the employer may implement the terms it already put on the table. That is not a punishment and it is not a loophole — it is the ordinary consequence of talks breaking down. The obligation to keep bargaining continues, but the employer’s terms are what people work under while it does.

The part worth sitting with A delay quietly moves the advantage to whoever is more comfortable with the status quo.

Why it gets worse, not steadier

The cost of a delay is not the delay.

A long negotiation does not just postpone an outcome. It changes the conditions the outcome gets decided under.

None of this requires anyone to behave badly. It is the ordinary physics of a long wait: attention drifts, information thins, and the people who have to live with the result start wanting it finished more than they want it right.

We cannot control how long bargaining takes. We can control whether a long one finds us organized or scattered — and that is decided before it starts, not during.

  1. 01

    The date passes

    Old terms hold. Nothing visible changes, so nothing feels urgent.

  2. 02

    Information thins out

    With no regular channel, members hear less and guess more.

  3. 03

    Patience becomes pressure

    People who cannot see progress start wanting it over, on almost any terms.

  4. 04

    The table notices

    A membership that wants it finished is a weaker hand than one that knows what it is holding out for.

Before January 1

What has to already exist if negotiations run long.

Every one of these is easier to build now than during a negotiation, and useless if we start building it after one goes sideways.

  1. 1

    A membership list that is actually current

    Every member, reachable, verified. A negotiation that runs long is one that depends on reaching the same people again and again, over months.

  2. 2

    A channel members already know

    One address where updates appear, on a cadence people can predict — including the weeks when the honest update is that nothing moved. If the first time members look for it is the week they need it, it is too late to establish.

  3. 3

    A plain-language guide to the current agreement

    You cannot judge what is being offered without knowing what you already hold. That guide takes time to write, and the time to write it is not during bargaining.

  4. 4

    A plan for the bad version

    What we do if talks stall or deadlock: how members are told, how quickly, and how we respond if terms are implemented over our objection.

None of this sits apart from the campaign priorities. It is those priorities, restated as what a long negotiation would actually demand of us.

Why this is not abstract

The agreement being negotiated is the one that sets your pay.

01

Know what you currently hold

The Employment Agreement decides pay, benefits, hours, and what happens when something goes wrong. It is the document worth reading before anyone proposes changing it.

02

Know what is being asked for

Both sides arrive with proposals. Members should be able to read what ours are, and what the employer’s are, written out in plain terms alongside the clause each one would change.

03

Keep the final say where it belongs

Nothing takes effect without a two-thirds vote of the membership in good standing. That is a real protection, and it only works if everyone voting has the offer and the current terms in front of them first.

The dates the documents actually set

The current agreement runs in full force through midnight on June 30, 2027. After that it renews itself year to year unless one party gives the other written notice to change or end it (Sec. 17.1).

That notice has to be given by one party and received by the other no later than May 2, 2027 — a received-by deadline, not a sent-by one — and by May 2 of every year it renews into after that. Miss it and the agreement rolls forward another full year unchanged. Notice to modify counts, so nobody has to threaten termination to get to the table (Sec. 17.1).

Our own bylaws add one more: the two-member negotiating committee has to be elected at a meeting held at least ninety days before the agreement expires — so no later than the start of April 2027 (Art. VIII.A).

Those are the deadlines. Opening talks on January 1 is what I am campaigning for, not something the agreement requires — and the case for it is simply that the three dates above arrive faster than an unprepared association can meet them.

Source: 2023–2027 APA/APSEA Agreement, Section 17.1; APSEA Constitution and Bylaws, Article VIII.A.

What I am asking for

We should not have to hope this one goes quickly.

A short negotiation would be good news. But a plan that only works if bargaining is short is not a plan. The association should be able to go the distance without losing the membership’s attention, understanding, or patience along the way.

That capacity does not appear when it is needed. It gets built beforehand — and the beforehand ends on the date I want talks open, January 1 — — days from today.
