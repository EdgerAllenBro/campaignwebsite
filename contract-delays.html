What happens during delays

Negotiations do not always move fast. When talks extend past the contract's expiration date, employees continue working under the old agreement while both sides bargain. But that status quo is only temporary — and the law draws a line.

Employees work under existing rules

Pay rates, benefits, work rules, scheduling provisions — everything stays as it was in the previous agreement. That agreement is still the controlling document; you are not working at will.

Wage increases and benefit changes pause

If a new agreement has been signed and negotiation is about what it says, scheduled raises, new benefits, or other monetary improvements do not take effect until the deal is ratified. Once ratified, changes are usually made retroactive to the contract start date — so people catch up all at once.

At impasse, the employer can act

If negotiators reach a legal impasse (good-faith deadlock over weeks of meetings), the employer may implement their final proposed contract terms. That is not a penalty or punishment — it is the legal remedy when negotiations break down. The union can still appeal to the NLRB or court, but the employer's terms take effect while that case proceeds.

Why timing is strategy

When the deadline approaches, the side with more to lose — or more leverage — can pressure the other. APSEA cannot control how long the employer takes, but we can control whether we are ready.

Delayed start = pressure on us

If APA postpones bargaining or stretches out early rounds, employees feel anxiety about their future. That anxiety weakens the union's position at the table. An association members have heard from before negotiations start — one they understand, one they trust — is harder to panic.

Ready infrastructure = steady ground

A clear, accessible database of who we represent and how to reach them. A website that explains our contract. Plain-language guides to the existing agreement so members know what they have to protect. These exist so we can organize and communicate while negotiations happen, not scramble to build them during talks.

Impasse preparation = real leverage

If negotiations stall and the employer implements changes, the union's power comes from membership unity and the ability to escalate. That requires a membership that knows what was offered, why it was rejected, and what the employer just took unilaterally. A prepared association can fight that. An unprepared one cannot.

What APSEA needs before January 1

Months before negotiations begin is the time to build. Not after a delay happens. Not after an impasse is declared. Before.

  1. 1 A working database: Every member in the unit, contact info, position, work location. Maintained, current, accurate. The starting point for everything else.
  2. 2 A public-facing presence: A website people can find when they search for APSEA. Information about who we are, who we represent, how to reach leadership. Not glossy. Clear and accessible.
  3. 3 Contract literacy: A plain-language guide to the existing Employment Agreement. What it covers, what it guarantees, where to look when something does not match. So members know what they are voting to protect or change.
  4. 4 A communication schedule: Regular updates during bargaining. Why proposals matter. How the timeline works. What the employer is asking for, and what we are asking for, in language everyone understands.
  5. 5 Contingency plans: If negotiations delay or deadlock. How to activate the membership. How to frame the employer's unilateral moves. How to appeal or contest them if they violate law or the prior agreement.

Your contract, your pay, your rules

Why this matters An Employment Agreement is not abstract. It decides your pay rate, your benefits, your work schedule, your rights when something goes wrong. If bargaining stalls and the employer implements changes before ratification, those changes affect you immediately — and possibly for years, until the dispute is resolved.

You should understand what your current agreement says. You should know what APA proposes to change. You should have a union leadership ready to negotiate hard and communicate clearly throughout.

That is what this campaign is about. Not aspirational. Not distant. The infrastructure that makes the difference when the table gets real.
