What I would actually do with the term

A term is three years. Here is what I would build in each one. And the real test is whether any of it survives year four.

Officers serve three years, running November 1 through October 31. That is long enough to build something real and short enough that anything depending on one person will not outlast it. So the plan below is sequenced deliberately: reach members first, give them something to use second, and spend the third year making sure none of it walks out the door when the term ends.

Start with year one How it is built See the campaign priorities

The sequence

Each year only works because the one before it happened.

There is no point building a member portal before we can reliably reach the members. No point running a survey we cannot compare to anything. No point documenting a process nobody has run yet. The order below is not a wish list in priority order — it is the order the work actually has to happen in.

  1. Year One — Foundation
  2. Year Two — Engagement
  3. Year Three — Sustainability
  4. Year Four — It holds without me

The standard I want to be judged against Not what I built, but what still works after somebody else is doing this job.

Year One

Foundation and trust

Year one opens with negotiations already on the calendar, which sets the priority for me: be able to reach every member, and give them somewhere reliable to look. You cannot keep people informed through a bargaining year if you are not certain you can contact them, and you cannot ask anyone to trust a process they cannot see any part of.

  1. 1

    A membership list that is actually current

    Every member, reachable, verified, and updatable without starting from scratch each time. Nothing else on this page functions if this does not exist first.

  2. 2

    A website with the basics on it

    Member resources, an FAQ, who the officers are and how to reach them, and an archive of meeting minutes. The answer to a routine question should be a link, not a rumor.

  3. 3

    A monthly update on a date you can predict

    Email and web, same cadence every month — including the months when the honest update is that nothing moved. A channel only becomes trusted by being boring first.

  4. 4

    A proposal tracker members can follow

    What was proposed, by whom, what has moved and what has not. Members should be able to follow the shape of a negotiation without having been in the room for it.

  5. 5

    Named contacts in each work area

    Volunteers in the departments where members are concentrated, so there is a person to ask and not only an address to write to. Questions people will ask a colleague are questions they will never send to an inbox.

  6. 6

    An annual member survey

    A baseline on priorities and satisfaction, run the same way each year so the answers can be compared to last year rather than just collected again.

  7. 7

    A presence where people already look

    A public professional listing at minimum, plus a private space for members only. One exists so outsiders can find that we are real; the other so members can talk without an audience.

  8. 8

    A full account once negotiations close

    After negotiations end, expect a full accounting in both writing and video: what we asked for, what we got, what we didn't, and the reasoning behind each trade-off.

Year Two

Engagement and continuity

Year one makes information available. Year two makes it usable, and turns the association into something you interact with rather than something you receive mail from. This is also the year to start proving the work does not depend on the people currently doing it.

  1. 1

    A member portal worth logging into

    The agreement itself, benefits information, and the current status of anything you have raised — available without having to ask a person first.

  2. 2

    Listening sessions on the calendar in advance

    The bylaws require two membership meetings a year (Art. VI.A). That is a governance floor, not a communications plan. Quarterly sessions, scheduled far enough ahead that people can arrange to be there.

  3. 3

    A contract education program

    Walking through what the Employment Agreement actually says, section by section, in language that does not require a translator — including what changed and what it means for your pay and your working conditions.

  4. 4

    Issue tracking, so problems do not evaporate

    Every issue raised gets logged with a status and a date. Nothing should disappear because the person who heard about it got busy or moved on.

  5. 5

    Succession planning and cross-training

    Every committee role gets a documented process and a second person who has actually done it. One person leaving should never be the reason something stops.

  6. 6

    Messaging that is not only text

    Short video and audio for the things that land better spoken. Some members will never read a long email, and that is a fact to design around rather than complain about.

  7. 7

    Outreach to allied unions and staff associations

    Other staff unions have solved problems we are about to hit for the first time. Knowing them before we need them is how a small association borrows credibility and experience it cannot generate alone.

  8. 8

    An annual report to the membership

    What was accomplished, what was not, and what is coming. The financial portion belongs to the officer who keeps those books — my part is making sure the report reaches you in a form you can actually read.

Year Three

Sophistication and sustainability

By year three the question stops being whether these things exist and becomes whether they survive a change of officers. That means preparing for the next contract cycle before it starts, and writing everything down well enough that the next person does not have to rebuild it from memory and guesswork.

  1. 1

    Early modeling for the next contract

    Proposals drafted and costed while there is still time to think, instead of assembled under deadline. The bylaws already front-load this: the negotiating committee is elected at least 90 days before expiry (Art. VIII.A).

  2. 2

    Engagement measured, not assumed

    Who reads updates, who attends, who votes. Not to monitor anyone — to find out which channels are actually working and stop spending effort on the ones that are not.

  3. 3

    A proposal for dedicated communications help

    Paid or volunteer, brought to the membership as a decision for you to make rather than an arrangement announced after the fact. By year three there should be evidence for whether the workload justifies it.

  4. 4

    A training pipeline for future officers

    Any active member in good standing is eligible to hold office. The point of a pipeline is that they can arrive already knowing how the work is done, instead of learning it during a negotiation.

  5. 5

    Relationships built before turnover

    The people on the other side of the table change. Knowing new counterparts before there is a dispute in front of us is cheaper than meeting them during one.

  6. 6

    Advocacy on what affects APSEA members

    Where legislation or industry practice reaches our members’ own pay, benefits, or job security, the association should have a considered position rather than finding out afterward.

  7. 7

    An archive that outlives its authors

    Past agreements, grievance outcomes, why decisions were made the way they were. Right now that history lives in individual memories, which is the same as saying it leaves when they do.

  8. 8

    Benchmarking against comparable unions

    What associations our size actually do, what it costs them, and which of it is worth copying. There is no prize for inventing a solution someone already published.

The part that actually matters

A plan that needs me is not a plan.

Most association projects do not fail. They just stop when the person carrying them moves on, and nobody can quite say when.

That is the failure mode worth designing against, because it is the one that has already happened here. A social media account that showed promise and went quiet did not fail on the merits — it ran out of the one person who was keeping it going.

So every item on this page has to answer a second question beyond whether it is useful: what happens to it when whoever built it is gone. If the honest answer is “it stops,” it is not finished yet.

  1. 01

    The term ends

    Which it does, on a known date, for every officer.

  2. 02

    The work is written down

    Every recurring task documented well enough for someone who was never told how.

  3. 03

    The next officer starts from here

    Not from zero, and not from whatever they can piece together afterward.

  4. 04

    Members never feel the seam

    The updates keep arriving. That is the whole measure of success.

The rules everything is built to

Four constraints that apply to every item above.

These are not aspirations. They are the reasons some obvious-sounding ideas are missing from this page — anything that fails these tends to work for about four months.

01

Asynchronous first

Members work different schedules. Anything that only works if you are free at a particular hour quietly excludes the people who are not, and they are the ones least likely to say so.

02

Low-maintenance by design

If a thing needs one specific person to survive, it is a hobby rather than infrastructure. Volunteer time is the scarcest resource the association has and every system should be built as though it will be maintained by someone busier than I am.

03

Written down, not remembered

Every recurring task gets documentation good enough for a stranger to follow. This is the least interesting item on the page and probably the most important one.

04

Measured, so we can be wrong

Participation, how long issues take to resolve, what members say when asked. Numbers you can check are how you discover something is not working while there is still time to change it.

One thing this page is not. The bylaws give the Vice President a narrow job: assume the President’s authority in their absence, preside when asked, and assist in the discharge of all duties (Art. VII). Most of what is listed above is work rather than authority — writing, publishing, documenting, organizing, and showing up — and that part I can commit to directly. Anything involving money, a new formal role, or a change to how the association is structured is not mine to decide. Those come to the membership as proposals, and you vote on them.

This is the campaign’s own plan, not an official APSEA program, and not a commitment made on the association’s behalf. Article references are to the APSEA Constitution and Bylaws, which govern if anything here differs from them.

What I am asking for

Three years is enough time to fix this permanently.

Not enough time to fix everything, and I would not claim otherwise. But it is enough to leave the association reachable, informed, and running on systems that do not depend on who happens to be holding the office — which is more than any single negotiation can deliver.

Year one starts with a negotiation already underway, and the groundwork for it has to be finished before the clock starts on January 1 — — days from today.
