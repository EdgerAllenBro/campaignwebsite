The visibility gap

Search for APSEA. You’ll find APA. And a social media account that showed promise, then went quiet.

The Allied Pilots Staff Employees Association represents the staff employed by the Allied Pilots Association — the people who make APA run. It has no public-facing website: no page that says who it represents, what it does, or how to reach it. You are reading a campaign page because the association page it should link to does not exist.

What the absence costs See the campaign priorities

What’s there now

Type our name into a search engine and you get somebody else’s union.

Not a competitor, and not a coincidence. APA is the organization our members are employed by, and it shares most of our acronym. That is exactly why the substitution is so complete: the employer is findable, and the people who run it are not.

Who runs into this

  • A member with a question No canonical place to check what the contract says, who represents them, or when they’ll hear something next.
  • Someone newly in the unit Nothing to read that explains what representation means or who to ask. They learn it secondhand, or they don’t.
  • An APA pilot Interacts with APSEA-represented staff constantly, and has no way to find out that the association exists.
  • Another union, or anyone looking in A sister local, a reporter, a labor organization sizing us up — all hit the same wall.

An organization that cannot be found is an organization that can be overlooked.

The baseline

A public website is table stakes in organized labor.

Nearly every union of any standing maintains one — including APA, our members’ employer. It is not a vanity project. It is how an association proves it exists, states what it stands for, and gives people a way in.

  • 01

    Identity

    A name, a purpose, and a plain statement of who the association represents.

  • 02

    Reachability

    Officers, contacts, and a route to representation that does not depend on knowing someone.

  • 03

    Record

    Governing documents and reference material in one findable, current place.

  • 04

    Standing

    Evidence, to anyone who looks, that this is an organized body and not an informality.

The cost

What the absence actually costs

Invisibility is not a neutral condition. It compounds, and it compounds against us.

An association nobody has heard of has a harder time recruiting engaged members, building leverage at the table, or getting taken seriously by the company. Every one of those follows from the same root: there is nothing to point at.

This is why I treat communications as organizational infrastructure rather than publicity. A website is not the goal. Being findable, credible, and answerable is the goal — and a website is the cheapest way to get there.

Contract negotiations begin January 1. The association’s public standing is part of what it brings to that table.

  1. 01

    Nothing to find

    No page, no listing, no address anyone recognizes.

  2. 02

    Nothing to join

    People who might engage have no obvious way in.

  3. 03

    Nothing to cite

    No public record of what the association says or stands for.

  4. 04

    Nothing to weigh

    An organization with no public footprint is easy for the other side to discount.

The fix

It does not need to be elaborate. It needs to exist.

Nobody is asking for a redesign every quarter. A handful of pages, accurate and current, at an address that belongs to the association, does almost all of the work.

  1. 01

    Who we represent

    One page, in plain terms, naming the work our members do and the unit the association covers.

  2. 02

    How to reach us

    Officers, roles, and a contact route that works whether or not you already know who to call.

  3. 03

    What governs us

    Bylaws and agreement references kept in one place, so the answer to “where does it say that” is a link.

  4. 04

    What’s happening

    Updates on a predictable schedule, at an address members recognize, especially during bargaining.

The takeaway A union that can’t be found is doing its members a disservice it doesn’t have to.

Who APSEA represents

APSEA is a non-profit, employee-representing association. Its members are the staff employed by the Allied Pilots Association — the people who administer the contract, run the systems, and keep the union operating day to day.

Membership is open to anyone actively employed by APA who is eligible under the bylaws, on the same terms regardless of race, sex, age, prior employment status, or which department they work in. Supervisors, managers, guards, and confidential employees are excluded, as defined by law.

Under its constitution the association exists to promote the interests of its members and protect their individual and collective rights; to establish and exercise the right of collective bargaining over rates of pay, rules, and working conditions; and to settle disputes and grievances promptly.

Members elect three officers — President, Vice President, and Secretary-Treasurer — each serving a three-year term that runs November 1 through October 31.

Source: APSEA Constitution and Bylaws, Articles I, II and VII.

The upside

What a presence opens up

Everything above is what the absence costs. This is the other half: things that are not available to an organization nobody can find, and that become straightforward once one can be.

  1. 01

    Working with other unions and labor organizations

    Labor runs on solidarity, and solidarity starts with being findable. Other locals, labor councils, and trade organizations reach out to groups they can identify, verify, and contact. The support that matters during a bargaining cycle — shared research, letters of support, coordinated pressure — comes out of relationships built well before anyone needs them. Every one of those relationships starts with somebody being able to look us up and see that we are real.

    What it takes A page that names who we represent, and an address that reaches an officer.

  2. 02

    Showing up in the community as APSEA

    Members already volunteer, donate, and turn out for causes they care about. They do it as individuals, because there is no association to do it as. A public presence lets that work happen in the association’s name — a group at a charity event, a sponsored team, a drive other members can join. The same effort then builds something the association keeps, instead of ending when the day does.

    What it takes Somewhere to say what we are doing and how to join in.

  3. 03

    Merchandise printed by union shops

    Shirts, patches, and printed material ordered from union print shops do three things at once. Members carry the association’s name into everyday view, which is visibility no newsletter buys. The spend goes to other union workers, which is the entire point of the union label. And the association gets something tangible — an identity people can wear, rather than one they have to be told about.

    What it takes A mark worth printing, and somewhere members can find and order it.

  4. 04

    A channel of our own

    If APSEA needs to say something publicly — correct a mischaracterization, state a position, answer the question that keeps coming up — it currently has nowhere to say it. Every statement depends on somebody else’s platform, their timing, and their framing. A site of our own means the association speaks in its own words, when it chooses to, and can point anyone to a single source that is actually ours.

    What it takes A page we control and can update the same day.

None of this needs permission. It needs an address.

One caveat

This page is a stopgap, and it should be replaced.

This is a campaign page. It does not speak for APSEA and it should not be mistaken for an official association publication. It exists because someone clicking “APSEA” deserved to land somewhere rather than nowhere.

The right outcome is that this page becomes unnecessary — because the association has its own site, at its own address, carrying the section above as its front page. Building that is part of what I’m campaigning for, and it is work that should be finished before negotiations open on January 1 — — days from today.
