Before Day One

The groundwork should be finished before the clock starts.

You should not hear from your association’s leadership for the first time in the middle of negotiations. Four things need to exist before January — not get assembled while bargaining is underway.

  1. 01

    Member contact database

    A maintained, accurate list of who the association represents and how to reach them. Without it, every other communication effort is guesswork.

  2. 02

    Dedicated APSEA email domain

    Official messages should arrive from an address members recognize on sight — not from whichever personal inbox happened to send it.

  3. 03

    Simple, reliable website

    One place that is always current, always reachable, and answers the questions members ask most. It does not need to be elaborate. It needs to work.

  4. 04

    Recurring member update cadence

    A schedule members can count on, established early enough that it is already routine by the time it matters most.

Infrastructure built under pressure is infrastructure built badly.

Priorities

What I’m Campaigning On

Five commitments, in the order they need to happen.

01

Priority One

Communications infrastructure, ready before day one

A real member contact database, a dedicated email domain, a simple website, and a recurring update cadence so nobody hears from leadership for the first time in the middle of negotiations. This groundwork needs to exist before January, not get built while the clock is running.

The takeaway Build it before bargaining begins.

02

Priority Two

Contract literacy the membership can trust

Every APSEA member works under an Employment Agreement that sets their pay, their rules, and their working conditions. Most people have never read it end to end, and there is no plain-language guide to what it actually says.

Reading dense agreement language is what I do professionally — contract administration on the APA/AA collective bargaining agreement — and that skill transfers straight across to ours. What I want to build is a plain-language guide to the APSEA Employment Agreement: what it covers, what it entitles you to, and where to look when something does not look right.

That matters year-round, not only at the table. A member who does not know what the agreement says cannot tell when it has been missed, and cannot raise it.

Why this is not optional Nothing takes effect without a two-thirds vote of the membership in good standing.

That is the bar our own bylaws set for ratification, and it is not a bar a confused membership clears. Everyone who votes has to understand what they are voting on, which makes plain-language explanation a requirement of the job rather than a courtesy (Art. VIII.B).

  • Plain language
  • Accurate information
  • No rumor mill
03

Priority Three

Member voice before the table, not after

A priorities survey before proposals get finalized, so our negotiating committee walks in with a real mandate. A plain-language FAQ for anyone who hasn’t been through a bargaining cycle before.

  1. Members
  2. Priorities Survey
  3. Negotiating Committee
  4. Bargaining Table
04

Priority Four

Predictable, honest updates during negotiations

A fixed cadence, clear lines on what’s confidential versus shareable, and one point of contact.

“No update this week” is still an update, and it beats silence every time.

Bargaining rules will always limit what can be shared. Predictability is what members can be given regardless — and it is what keeps speculation from filling the gap.

05

Priority Five

Making APSEA visible, not just active

Most pilots interact with APSEA-represented staff constantly and couldn’t tell you our association exists. Most of the broader community has never heard of us at all.

I want a real public-facing presence: a website that explains who we are and what we do, regular visibility with APA membership so pilots know who’s behind the work they rely on, and a presence in the wider labor and aviation community so APSEA is recognized as organized and serious.

The argument

Why this matters

An association nobody’s heard of has a harder time recruiting engaged members, building leverage at the table, or getting taken seriously by the employer.

Communications are not newsletters and social posts. They are organizational infrastructure — how an association knows its own members, proves it represents them, and shows it can act as one body.

There is a hard number attached to this. Nothing we negotiate takes effect until two-thirds of the membership in good standing votes to ratify it. Clearing that bar is a communications problem before it is a bargaining one.

This is groundwork that pays off well past this election cycle — in what the next Employment Agreement says about your pay, your rules, and your working conditions.

  1. 01

    Communication

    Members reliably receive accurate information.

  2. 02

    Engagement

    Informed members participate, respond, and show up.

  3. 03

    Credibility

    An association that speaks for its members can prove it.

  4. 04

    Leverage

    Credibility is what the other side of the table has to account for.

Why I’m running

Experience that translates to the job

I work contract administration on the APA/AA collective bargaining agreement side.

That work is the daily practice of reading complex agreement language, determining what it actually requires, and explaining it accurately to people who need to act on it. It is less about opinion and more about precision: what the contract says, what it does not say, and where the difference matters.

It is the same skill APSEA needs during a negotiation cycle — the ability to take dense proposal language and give members a clear, honest account of what is on the table, without overstating progress and without leaving people to guess.

I am running because that capability should be paired with communications infrastructure that actually reaches people. Accurate information nobody receives is not much better than no information at all.

The standard

What you should be able to expect from your Vice President

The office, as the bylaws define it

“The Vice President shall assume the authority and responsibility of the President in the absence or incapacitation of the latter. She/he shall also preside when called upon by the President… He shall assist the President in the discharge of all duties.”

APSEA Constitution and Bylaws, Article VII

That is the whole of the job description. The bylaws set what the office must do; they do not say how well. The four commitments below are mine — but each one answers something the governing document actually requires.

  • 01

    Preparation

    Infrastructure should be ready before it is urgently needed.

    Why the office requires it The Vice President has to be able to assume the President’s authority “in the absence or incapacitation of the latter” — without notice. And the bylaws already front-load the bargaining timeline: the negotiating committee must be elected at a meeting held at least 90 days before the agreement expires (Art. VII; Art. VIII.A).

  • 02

    Clarity

    Members deserve plain-language explanations of complicated issues.

    Why the office requires it No agreement with APA takes effect until it is ratified by a two-thirds majority of the membership in good standing. That bar cannot be cleared by members who are guessing at what they are voting on — and the Vice President is one of the three officers who then sign it (Art. VIII.B).

  • 03

    Consistency

    Communication should happen on a predictable schedule.

    Why the office requires it The bylaws require a minimum of two membership meetings a year, one in the second quarter and one in the fourth. That is a governance floor, not a communications plan — and in a bargaining year it is nowhere near enough on its own (Art. VI.A).

  • 04

    Access

    Members should know where to ask questions and where reliable information comes from.

    Why the office requires it Members elect the negotiating committee from the floor, and any officer can be recalled on a petition of one-third of members and a two-thirds vote. Both are real powers, and both depend on members knowing who to reach (Art. VIII.A; Art. VII.D).

Every commitment above exists to make the powers the bylaws already grant you usable.

For members

How the election actually works

Most of us have never been through one of these, and nobody really explains it. Here it is in plain terms — which is the whole argument of this campaign, applied to the campaign itself.

  1. 1

    Nominations come from the floor

    They are made at a regular meeting, not by form or email. At most three nominees per office go on the ballot.

  2. 2

    The vote is by secret ballot

    If an office ends up with only one nominee, the vote may be taken by a show of hands instead.

  3. 3

    Winning takes a majority

    Fifty percent plus one of the votes cast. If nobody clears it, there is a run-off between the two candidates with the most votes.

  4. 4

    Every active member in good standing votes

    In person or by proxy. Any active member is also eligible to hold office — including you.

Officers serve three years, running November 1 through October 31. This is the campaign’s plain-language summary of Articles VII.B, VII.C and XI of the APSEA Constitution and Bylaws, not an official election notice. The bylaws are the authority, and the association’s own meeting notice is where dates and details come from. If anything here differs from either, they govern.

The commitment

APSEA should enter January organized, informed, visible, and ready.

This campaign is about durable infrastructure — a database that stays current, a domain members recognize, a site that stays accurate, and a schedule people can rely on. Those things outlast any single negotiation, and they outlast this election.

If that is the association you want going into bargaining, I’d like your support.
